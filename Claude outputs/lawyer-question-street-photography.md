# Question for legal counsel: street photography and Ghana's Data Protection Act

## Background

I am a fine art and documentary photographer based in Accra, trading as Jeffery Asare. Part of my practice is candid street and documentary photography: unposed photographs of people in public spaces (markets, streets, public events), taken without asking for permission at the moment, in keeping with standard documentary photography practice.

Some of these images are later selected, printed as limited-edition fine art prints, and offered for sale through my website and occasionally through galleries or exhibitions. This work is not used in advertising, is not used to endorse any product or service, and is not attributed to or promoted using any subject's name; subjects are typically unidentified in the way the work is presented.

Separately, for portrait, wedding, and commissioned work, I already use signed model releases, since those subjects are directly engaged and agree to specific uses in advance. This question relates only to the candid street/documentary side of the work.

## What I'd like clarity on

1. Does Ghana's Data Protection Act, 2012 (Act 843) treat a photograph of an identifiable person, taken in a public place without their consent, as "personal data" being "processed" in a way that requires a legal basis (such as consent or registration as a data controller), even when the purpose is artistic/documentary rather than commercial or advertising?

2. Many jurisdictions draw a distinction between editorial/artistic use (generally permitted without consent) and commercial/advertising use (generally requiring consent), sometimes under a "journalistic, literary or artistic purposes" exemption. Does Ghanaian law recognise a similar distinction, and if so, does selling a limited-edition print of a street photograph as fine art fall on the artistic side of that line, or does the act of selling it push it toward "commercial"?

3. Am I required to register as a data controller with Ghana's Data Protection Commission for this kind of work, and if so, what would that involve in practice?

4. If someone identifiable in one of my street photographs objects after the fact and asks that an image not be sold or displayed, what are my actual legal obligations at that point: is there a duty to comply, or is it a matter of goodwill rather than legal requirement?

5. Is there anything practical I should be doing now, such as a privacy notice, a documented process for handling objections, or limits on how I select or caption images, that would meaningfully reduce risk, short of obtaining releases for street subjects (which is not practical for this kind of work)?

## Why this matters to me

I want to keep working in this tradition honestly and respectfully, and I'd rather understand the real legal picture in Ghana now than find out the hard way later. I'm less interested in a maximally cautious answer than in an accurate one: what the law actually requires versus what is simply good practice.
